## Part I: Relativity

Greene's explanation of special and general relativity is one of the clearest I've read. Key points:

- **Special relativity**: the speed of light is constant for all observers. Time slows down and space contracts for objects moving near light speed. The famous `E = mc²` falls out of this.
- **General relativity**: gravity isn't a force — it's the curvature of spacetime caused by mass. Massive objects literally bend the fabric of the universe.

The bowling-ball-on-a-trampoline analogy is overused, but Greene adds nuance: it's not just _space_ that warps, it's **spacetime**. Clocks tick slower in stronger gravitational fields. GPS satellites have to account for this.

> Einstein's insight: the laws of physics must look the same to all observers, regardless of their motion. Everything else follows from this single principle.

## Part II: Quantum Mechanics

The quantum world is deeply weird, and Greene doesn't shy away from it:

- **Wave-particle duality**: electrons and photons behave as both waves and particles depending on how you observe them
- **The uncertainty principle**: you _cannot_ simultaneously know both the position and momentum of a particle with perfect precision. This isn't a measurement limitation — it's fundamental.
- **Quantum entanglement**: two particles can be correlated such that measuring one instantly affects the other, regardless of distance. Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance."

The core tension: general relativity describes gravity beautifully at large scales. Quantum mechanics describes the other three forces perfectly at small scales. But they are **mathematically incompatible**. When you try to apply quantum mechanics to gravity, the equations produce infinities.

## Part III: String Theory

This is where it gets speculative. The core claim:

1. The fundamental building blocks aren't point particles — they're tiny, vibrating **strings** of energy
2. Different vibration patterns correspond to different particles (electrons, quarks, photons, gravitons)
3. The math requires **10 or 11 dimensions** — the extra ones are "compactified" (curled up too small to observe)
4. String theory naturally includes gravity, potentially resolving the relativity/quantum conflict

> [!TIP]
> The first two-thirds of this book are a masterclass in science communication. Worth reading for the relativity and quantum chapters alone, even if you never care about strings.

> [!WARNING]
> **No experimental evidence.** The strings are predicted to be at the Planck scale (~10⁻³⁵ meters), far beyond anything we can probe. The "landscape problem" — 10⁵⁰⁰ possible configurations — remains unresolved.
