<!-- wp:mark 114e8a4f-1188-493a-9644-b08d7a315637 -->
Harari's grand narrative of human history, from the cognitive revolution through the agricultural revolution to the present. The central thesis — that Homo sapiens conquered the world because we can cooperate flexibly in large numbers through shared fictions like money, religion, and nations — is the kind of framework that recolors everything once you see it.

<!-- wp:mark af7f48dd-9f2c-4680-a0b9-c38bcbd04d77 -->
The agricultural revolution chapter ("History's Biggest Fraud") is the most provocative section. The argument that farming made most individual humans worse off while making the species more successful is well-constructed, even if the counterfactual is impossible to prove.

<!-- wp:mark 88b0c3f9-f623-4a0b-90d2-93e84612df35 -->
It's a broad book, and breadth means some sections get painted with too wide a brush. Specialists in any given period will find things to quibble with. But as a synthesis that forces you to zoom out and think about what "progress" actually means, it delivers. The last few chapters on the future of the species feel more speculative and less grounded than the historical material.
